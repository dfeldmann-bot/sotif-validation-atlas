00Before you start: six ideas

This page needs no statistics beyond these six ideas. Each takes a minute.

1 · Reading very small numbersSafety targets are tiny, so they are written as powers of ten. 10⁻⁶ is 0.000001, one in a million. 10⁻⁷ is one in ten million. 5·10⁻⁷ per hour is 0.0000005 per hour. The quick way to read a rate: 1 divided by it is the waiting time. 1 ÷ (5·10⁻⁷) = 2,000,000, so “once every 2 million hours”.
2 · Rates and chancesA rate counts per hour (“4 crossings per hour”). A chance is per event (“fails in 1 of 10 million crossings”). Rate × chance is a new rate: 4 × 1/10,000,000 = 4 failures per 10 million hours. Most of this page is that one multiplication.
3 · Chains of chances multiplyHarm needs several things to go wrong in a row. If each link has a chance, multiply them: 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.8 = 0.2, so 1 in 5.
4 · You cannot measure “zero”, only bound itNo failures in a test does not prove there are none, only that they are rare. The rule of three: if nothing happened in n tries or hours, the rate is very likely below 3 ÷ n. Why 3? If 3 events were expected, seeing none happens only 5 % of the time.
5 · “95 % confident” and upper boundsCounts vary by luck, so we never trust the plain average. We use an upper bound: the most pessimistic value that still fits the data. “95 % confident” means this way of calculating lands above the true value at least 95 times in 100.
6 · Test dangerous cases more, weigh them lessMost crossings are easy. The hard ones are simulated more often than they happen on real roads, and each such result counts for less, like a survey that interviews extra people in a small town and weights their answers down.

02Hazard identification and risk evaluationCl. 6.3–6.4

ISO 21448 borrows the hazard analysis method of ISO 26262 but asks a different question: not “what if a component fails?” but “what if the intended function is not good enough?”.

In plain wordsA hazardous behaviour is what the car does, described without the setting: not yielding, braking for no reason. It becomes dangerous only where someone is exposed, such as a pedestrian stepping onto a zebra crossing. That combination is the hazardous event. One behaviour has several hazardous events, each hurting a different kind of road user.Maths behind it: Chain rule · On the map: Hazard identification

03Acceptance criterion for the residual riskCl. 6.5

WhoSafety lead with the accident-data team, reviewed by the assessorWhenConcept phase, before any testingInputHuman accident data for the harm class, matched to the ODD

The harm class of the hazardous event decides which accident data the criterion is built on: pedestrian injuries for HE-1.1. Three decisions turn the human rate into the criterion.

  1. Human reference. How often human drivers injure pedestrians per hour of driving in a matched ODD (road type, speed, time of day). Here a synthetic placeholder: 50 injuries in 10 million hours, h = 5.0·10⁻⁶ per hour. Replace it with GIDAS or national data matched to the ODD.
  2. Positive risk balance with margin. Ten times better than humans for pedestrian injuries: 5.0·10⁻⁷ per hour for the whole harm class.
  3. Share for this hazardous event. Other behaviours and events also injure pedestrians (unintended acceleration near crossings, turning conflicts, low-speed manoeuvres). HE-1.1 gets 20 % of the pedestrian budget. The shares of all hazardous events in a harm class must add up to at most 100 %.
\req{A_H} = \text{share}\times\text{margin}\times\wld{h} = 0.2\times 0.1\times 5.0\cdot10^{-6} = \req{1.0\cdot 10^{-7}}\ \text{h}^{-1}

Source Acceptance criterion and positive risk balance: ISO 21448:2022, 3.1, p. 2, and 6.5, pp. 30–31 checked; budgets per incident type must add up: Warg et al. 2020, Sec. III-B location not checked; human rate, margin and share are inputs of this example derived here.

In plain wordsHuman drivers in this kind of traffic injure a pedestrian about 5 times per million hours (placeholder). We want 10 times better: 5 per 10 million hours. This crossing situation gets one fifth of that, because other mistakes can hurt pedestrians too: 1 per 10 million hours.On the map: Acceptance criterion
From the literature: plausibility check

Kröger (2025) proposes about 4·10⁻⁸ fatalities or serious injuries per hour for automated vehicles. The pedestrian class budget here, 5·10⁻⁷ per hour for injuries of any severity, is 12.5 times that. It fits only if fewer than 1 in 12.5 of these injuries are serious or fatal, before other harm classes are counted. A full case sets one criterion per severity class (Werling et al. 2025).

Assumption to defendThe human reference must be matched to the ODD: road type, speed, time of day, region. An unmatched benchmark is the first thing an assessor will challenge.

04From harm rate to behaviour rateAnnex C.2.1

WhoSafety leadWhenConcept phaseInputField studies for each link of the chain

The software only controls whether the behaviour happens. The rest of the chain is the world. Dividing by the chain gives how often the behaviour itself may occur:

\req{R_{HB}} = \frac{\req{A_H}}{\wld{P(E\mid HB)}\;\wld{P(\bar C\mid E)}\;\wld{P(S\mid \bar C)}} = \frac{1.0\cdot 10^{-7}}{0.5\cdot 0.5\cdot 0.8} = \req{5.0\cdot 10^{-7}}\ \text{h}^{-1}

Source ISO 21448:2022, C.2.1, Formulas (C.1)–(C.2), pp. 126–127 checked; full conditioning: Putze et al. 2023, Sec. IV-B(b) checked.

In plain wordsOnly 1 mistake in 5 leads to an injury (0.5 × 0.5 × 0.8 = 0.2). If injuries may happen once per 10 million hours, the mistake may happen five times as often: once per 2 million hours.Maths behind it: Chain rule · Rates × chances

In words: in zebra-crossing situations the robotaxi may fail to yield at most once every two million hours of driving. Everything from here on is about showing that this holds.

05The naive validation target, and why it failsAnnex C.3

The simplest validation target: drive until you have seen zero hazardous behaviours for long enough. With confidence \cnf{C}=0.95:

\wld{\tau} = \frac{\ln\!\big(1/(1-\cnf{C})\big)}{\req{R_{HB}}} = \frac{\ln 20}{5.0\cdot10^{-7}} = \frac{3.0}{5.0\cdot10^{-7}} = 6.0\cdot 10^{6}\ \text{h}

Source ISO 21448:2022, C.3, Formula (C.7), p. 137 checked, taken from Littlewood & Wright 1997 checked; the same calculation in Kalra & Paddock 2016, Eq. 2, p. 3 checked.

In plain wordsIf the car really made the mistake once per 2 million hours, then in 6 million hours you would expect 3 mistakes. Seeing none when 3 are expected happens only 5 % of the time. So 6 million mistake-free hours show, with 95 % confidence, that the rate is low enough.Maths behind it: Poisson process · Zero-failure bound

A fleet of 100 robotaxis driving 20 hours a day collects 2,000 hours per day. Six million hours is 3,000 days, about 8.2 years, and every software release changes the car. ISO 21448 itself notes that this target can be impractical (C.3, Note 1).

From the literature: no shortcut replaces the field entirely

Betschinske et al. (2025) review the routes to less field testing: decomposition, sequential tests, Bayesian priors, importance sampling, extreme-value analysis and scenario-based testing. Each reduces the effort; none fully removes the need for field data. This example uses decomposition, importance sampling and scenario-based testing, and keeps field driving for exposure and completeness.

07Verification and validation strategyCl. 9

WhoSafety lead with the data, scenario and simulation teams; approved by the assessorWhenPilot phase, frozen before confirmatory testingWork productV&V strategy with validation targets (9.4)

Clause 9 asks for a strategy that covers the relevant scenario space and sets validation targets. Because the naive target is impractical, the strategy splits the claim into parts that can each be shown. ISO 21448 allows several validation targets for one acceptance criterion (3.33, Note 3).

a · Logical scenarios derived from the hazardous event

The hazardous event says what to test. Logical scenarios say how: each is a scenario structure with parameter ranges (Menzel et al. 2018). The logical scenarios of a hazardous event must not overlap and must cover it completely, because each will hold its own share of the budget.

LS-1Pedestrian approaches the crossing in clear viewego speed 20–50 km/h; pedestrian speed 0.8–2.5 m/s; time to arrival 0.5–5 s; light; road; pedestrian size
LS-2Pedestrian emerges from behind a stationary vehicle at the crossingas LS-1, plus occluder type (car, van, bus) and lateral gap
LS-3Robotaxi turns into the street and meets a pedestrian on the crossingturning speed 10–30 km/h; pedestrian position on the crossing; light; road; pedestrian size

Light, road surface and pedestrian size are parameters inside each logical scenario, not separate scenarios. Triggering conditions are regions of this parameter space: they steer the targeted cases (step 08) and the importance sampling (step 09), but they do not hold their own budget.

c · Budget per logical scenario

The budget split decides how much risk each group of road users carries, and through that the validation targets. It is therefore set by a safety principle, not by test cost or test effort. A cost- or effort-optimal split would accept more risk exactly where testing is harder.

  1. Reserve for unknown scenarios. Keep 10 % of the tolerable rate for situations nobody has listed (Clause 11): \req{b_{\text{unk}}}=5\cdot10^{-8}, leaving \req{B}=4.5\cdot10^{-7} per hour.
  2. Equal protection per encounter. Every pedestrian at this zebra crossing gets the same protection, whichever logical scenario they are in. So every logical scenario gets the same allowed failure probability per encounter, and its budget share follows its exposure.
  3. Human baseline cap. No logical scenario may end up worse than human drivers in that same scenario.
  4. Freeze. Shares are fixed in the V&V plan before the confirmatory campaign and may only move between campaigns, with fresh evidence (Werling et al. 2025, Remark 3.7).
\req{b_k}=\req{B}\,\frac{\wld{\lambda_k^U}}{\sum_j\wld{\lambda_j^U}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \req{p^{*}}=\frac{\req{b_k}}{\wld{\lambda_k^U}}=\frac{\req{B}}{\sum_j\wld{\lambda_j^U}}=\frac{4.5\cdot10^{-7}}{5.065}=8.88\cdot10^{-8}\quad\text{for every }k

Source Linked scenario-class criteria add up to the hazard-based criterion, one validation target per scenario class, split to avoid concentrating residual risk: Hofmeister & Kinalzyk (AVL), Sec. 2.2 location not checked; several validation targets for one criterion: ISO 21448:2022, 3.33, Note 3, p. 10 checked; reserve: 11.1, p. 50 checked; equal protection per encounter as the allocation rule derived here.

e · Can the simulator be trusted?

The same scenario runs on the proving ground with a pedestrian dummy and in simulation. The safety metric \tst{y} is the distance between robotaxi and pedestrian when the pedestrian reaches the conflict zone; the difference is the simulator error.

\tst{e} = \tst{y_{\text{sim}}} - \wld{y_{\text{real}}},\qquad \text{pass}\iff \tst{y_{\text{sim}}}\ge\req{y_{\text{crit}}}+\tst{\delta},\qquad \tst{\delta}=\hat\mu+\cnf{z}\,\tst{\sigma^U}=0.05+6.5\cdot0.173=1.17\ \text{m}

Source Simulation accuracy is part of the safety argument: ISO 21448:2022, C.1, p. 125 checked; upper bound on σ via χ²: Casella & Berger 2002, Thm 5.3.1 location not checked; the margin rule derived here. Maths: normal tails.

In plain wordsThe simulator is usually off by about 0.15 m. Requiring a 1.17 m gap means the simulator would have to be wrong by 6.5 times its typical error before a real collision could hide behind a simulated pass. Under the usual bell curve that happens 4 times in 100 billion, which is 0.05 % of the allowed failure probability.Maths behind it: Normal tails
Assumption to defendNobody can measure a Gaussian tail at 6.5σ with 180 pairs. Place pairs near the failure boundary, check the error does not grow there, and do it for each triggering condition.

09Evaluation of unknown scenariosCl. 11

WhoSimulation and data teamsWhenConfirmatory campaign, on the frozen releaseWork productValidation results and residual-risk evaluation (11.4)

Clause 11 asks for the residual risk due to scenarios nobody has listed. Three kinds of evidence answer it.

a · Scenario exploration: where does it nearly fail?

An optimiser or adaptive sampler searches each logical scenario for the smallest clearance and maps the failure boundary. In the pilot it found the closest calls at night, at 40 km/h and more, with pedestrians reaching the conflict zone within 1.5 s; wet roads made it worse, which added TC-3 to the list. Exploration turns unknown hazardous scenarios into known ones, and it builds the test density q used for importance sampling. It is not a risk estimate: it over-samples danger on purpose.

\wld{x}^{*}=\arg\min_{\wld{x}\in\mathcal X_k}\ \tst{y_{\text{sim}}}(\wld{x}),\qquad \partial F_k=\{\wld{x}:\ \tst{y_{\text{sim}}}(\wld{x})=\req{y_{\text{crit}}}+\tst{\delta}\}

Source Iterative V&V turns area 3 into area 2: ISO 21448:2022, C.3, Fig. C.4, p. 135 checked; scenario exploration and random scenario simulation: 11.3, Table 11, p. 51 checked; search methods: Zhao et al. 2017 checked, Feng et al. 2023 checked, Moss et al. 2023 checked.

b · Random scenario simulation: how often does it fail?

Concrete scenarios are drawn from q, weighted by p/q, and run on the frozen release: 9.1 million runs, zero failures. The bound on the failure probability per encounter, including the simulator miss term:

\sys{p_k^U} = \frac{\cnf{L}\,\tst{w_k}}{\tst{n_k}} + \cnf{\Phi}(-\cnf{z}) = 8.88\cdot10^{-8}\quad\text{in every logical scenario}

Source Importance-sampling estimate: Owen 2013, Ch. 9 checked; ISO 21448:2022, C.5, pp. 149–151 checked; zero-failure logic: C.3, p. 137 checked; adding Φ(−z) as a miss term derived here. Maths: Bernoulli trials, change of measure.

c · Field driving: exposure and discovery

20,000 confirmatory fleet hours on the same release give fresh, tighter exposure bounds: 4.04, 0.513 and 0.310 per hour. They also feed the discovery log: no new kind of situation appeared, which bounds how often unlisted situations occur.

\wld{\lambda_{\text{new}}^U}=\frac{\cnf{L}}{\wld{\tau}}=\frac{5.08}{20\,000}=2.5\cdot10^{-4}\ \text{h}^{-1},\qquad \wld{\lambda_{\text{new}}^U}\cdot\sys{p_{\text{new}}}=2.5\cdot10^{-8}\le\req{b_{\text{unk}}}=5\cdot10^{-8}

Source Residual risk of unknown scenarios: ISO 21448:2022, 11.1, p. 50, and 11.3 Note 1, p. 52 checked; missing mass: Good 1953 location not checked, Berend & Kontorovich 2013, Theorem 1 checked; pnew ≤ 10⁻⁴ argued from the functional restriction of step 06 derived here. Maths: zero-failure bound, Good–Turing.

In plain wordsIf 20,000 hours brought nothing new, new kinds of situation must be rare: at most about once in 4,000 hours. The car cannot be tested in situations nobody has thought of, so the design rule of step 06 (slow to 10 km/h near anything unclassified at a crosswalk) argues that it fails in at most 1 of 10,000 of them. That uses half the reserve.Maths behind it: Good–Turing

What if one run had failed?

One failure in LS-1 multiplies its bound by 1.41: the LS-1 term becomes 5.07·10⁻⁷ and the sum 6.06·10⁻⁷. One failure in LS-2 or LS-3 would still pass (4.76·10⁻⁷ and 4.69·10⁻⁷); they fail at 3 and 5 failures. The most frequent scenario is the most sensitive, because its allowed failure probability is spread over the most encounters.

With one LS-1 failure: criterion not shown
Running extra simulations until the bound fits again is not allowed: the stopping rule would then depend on the result. Analyse the failure, add its cause to the triggering conditions (Clause 7), fix the function (Clause 8), and run a new frozen campaign on the new release.

After release the fleet keeps counting. At the limit, 730,000 fleet hours a year would show on average 0.365 events of HB-1 in zebra-crossing situations. Two rules decide when that count shows the criterion is violated and triggers the SOTIF issue process (13.4):

  • Fixed one-year rule: 3 or more events in one year show a violation at 95 % confidence. It is valid only for one look at the end of the year.
  • Anytime-valid rule: a likelihood-ratio e-process against twice the limit raises the alarm at 5 events within the first year, 6 within two, 7 within five. It stays valid however often it is checked.

13What an assessor will press on

  1. Hazard identification. Is the list of hazardous behaviours complete at vehicle level, and does each have hazardous events for every harm class it can cause?
  2. The benchmark and shares. Is the human reference matched to the ODD, and do the shares of all hazardous events in a harm class add up to at most 100 %? Is there one criterion per severity class?
  3. The chain. Are P(E | HB), P(C̄ | E) and P(S | C̄) the same in every logical scenario? If not, use per-scenario chains (Putze et al. 2023).
  4. Logical scenarios. Do they cover HE-1.1 completely without overlap? Is the discovery curve flat enough to justify the reserve?
  5. Budget principle. Why this split? Equal protection per encounter is defensible to the public; a split driven by test cost or effort is not.
  6. The simulator. Does the error model hold out at 6.5σ, for every triggering condition?
  7. Independence and freezing. Are runs independent concrete scenarios? Was the plan frozen before the confirmatory data, and all evidence collected on one release?

Glossary

ODD (operational design domain)
The roads, speeds, times and weather the robotaxi is allowed to drive in.
Hazardous behaviour
What the vehicle does wrong, described without a situation: does not yield, unintended braking, acceleration or steering, loss of braking, steering or propulsion.
Operational situation
The setting the vehicle is in, such as approaching a zebra crossing with a pedestrian about to cross.
Hazardous event
A hazardous behaviour in an operational situation where someone is exposed.
Acceptance criterion
The safety goal as a number: how often harm may occur at most.
Functional insufficiency
A weakness of the intended function, such as late detection.
Triggering condition
A circumstance that activates a functional insufficiency: darkness, occlusion, rain.
Logical scenario
A scenario structure with parameter ranges, derived from a hazardous event; it holds one share of the budget and gets one validation target.
Concrete scenario
A logical scenario with one value for every parameter: one test case.
Validation target
How much evidence is needed to show the criterion, such as “5.7 million turning runs with no failure”.
Exposure
How often a logical scenario occurs per hour of driving.
Upper bound
The most pessimistic value that still fits the data.
Bonferroni
Making each of several claims extra safe so that all of them together are still right 95 % of the time.
Importance factor
How much one simulation run counts compared with one real encounter, set by how much the test density over-samples dangerous variants.
Protection ratio
The largest allowed failure probability per encounter divided by the smallest; 1 means every encounter is protected alike.
Proving ground
A closed test track, here with a pedestrian dummy.
Frozen plan
The V&V plan fixed and approved before the confirmatory tests start.
Assessor
An independent expert, for example from TÜV, who checks the argument.
